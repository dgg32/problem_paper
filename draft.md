Papers at Risk: Retraction Prognosis for Academic Publications
==============
***Using a knowledge graph of the published literature and AI to flag risky publications early***

Science moves forward by standing on the shoulders of past work. Every published paper serves as a Jenga block in that ever-rising tower. But when a paper contains flawed data, invalid conclusions, or outright fraud, the flaw pollutes downstream science and acts like a compromised block. If it is not swiftly identified and pulled out, more and more research built on top risks crumbling. The median delay before retraction is close to two years, and throughout that window, unsuspecting researchers keep citing the flawed work, propagating it deeper into the literature.

The scientific community is not entirely defenseless against this threat, but its safeguards are stationed at extreme ends of the process, and neither can scale to match the volume and speed of retraction-worthy papers. At one end is the Retraction Watch database, which now logs more than 71,000 records and has become the field’s system of record. It is indispensable, but it is a ledger of verdicts rather than suspicions. So by the time a paper appears in it, the harm is already done. At the other end are independent sleuths, exemplified by figures like Geng Hongwei, known online by his handle “耿同学” (“Classmate Geng”), who left his PhD program in 2025 to become a full-time research-integrity whistleblower. He draws attention to data anomalies, prompting universities and journals to open formal investigations into suspect publications. Crowdsourced platforms like PubPeer serve a similar function. They give anonymous experts a place to flag image manipulation and statistical anomalies directly on a paper’s page. But sleuths face two fundamental limitations. Manual review cannot keep pace with the sheer volume of modern publishing, and whistleblowers often pay a steep price for stepping forward, making the approach fundamentally unsustainable. What is missing is an automated, scalable early-warning system, one that watches the literature at the pace it is actually produced and raises the alarm before the harm is done.

This project, Papers at Risk (Figure 1), is a step toward closing that gap, covering the parts of the literature where enough public metadata is available. It builds a knowledge graph of retracted and not-yet-retracted papers, runs the latter through a set of sensors, and scores each one’s retraction risk. Those scores give human sleuths a clear set of leads to investigate further, and an integrated AI audit tool is on hand to help. All of this happens before a formal retraction notice ever appears. The code for this project is hosted on my GitHub repository here.

## 1. Architecture, data, and author clustering

Papers at Risk consists of a graph and a series of scripts and is organized as two layers. The fact layer is a Neo4j knowledge graph. It stores only verifiable facts about papers, journals, institutions, and authors, sourced directly from places like Retraction Watch, OpenAlex, PubMed, the Federal Register, and DOAJ. The graph is then extended with candidate papers for risk scoring. They are not-yet-retracted papers from authors with a heavy retraction history. A second layer, the inference layer, sits on top of the fact layer and runs in two rounds. The first round clusters author identities, then runs the metadata-based sensors and the scoring engine to produce an initial risk score for each paper. A reviewer then selects the highest-scoring candidates for a second round, where the content-based sensors analyze the full texts and the AI audit tool PaperConan scans the data tables. Because both depend on access to the paper’s full text or data, this second round can only reach a small subset of the candidate papers, a limitation section 2 comes back to. The inference layer’s parameters, including clustering thresholds, sensor weights, and scoring methods, can all be tuned, so its outputs are subject to change as the pipeline is recalibrated. Together, the two scoring rounds give a reviewer a multi-faceted prognosis of a paper’s retraction risk.

For this proof of concept, the graph is seeded from Retraction Watch’s 2,100 Microbiology records, which after data-hygiene checks yield around 1,300 retracted seed papers. The pipeline then looks at the 24 authors with the heaviest retraction history. For each one, it pulls that author’s most-cited not-yet-retracted work from OpenAlex and PubMed. Those papers and their citation edges are added to the graph as candidate papers (Figure 3). The result is a working graph of roughly 2,000 papers today. Those 2,100 Microbiology records are only a small fraction of the broader biology subject, which runs up to 24,000 records. The architecture is designed to scale to the full dataset.

The literature graph lets the system ask complex questions that actually drive a paper’s risk score: “does this paper cite a paper that has already been retracted?” and “does this paper share an author-identity cluster with someone whose other paper was retracted for misconduct?” Answering either one means walking several hops, citation, then authorship, then identity, then retraction reason, in one query without a chain of joins.

One real case answers both at once. The U.S. Office of Research Integrity found that Dr. Shin-Hee Kim personally falsified and fabricated data across seven publications. The graph records her authorship of those seven papers, and also of one not-yet-retracted paper titled “Reverse Genetics for Newcastle Disease Virus as a Vaccine Vector.” Figure 4 shows that this candidate paper cites one of the papers retracted for misconduct, and that the graph’s author-identity clustering links both papers to the same Shin-Hee Kim.


That link is compelling evidence, but the graph never labels a person as fraudulent. The fact layer stops at facts like “this paper was retracted on this date for this reason” and “this author wrote this paper.” All are sourced from Retraction Watch and others.

But “this author wrote this paper” hides a real complication: different people can share the same name. In one extreme case, eleven distinct researchers are all named Ying Zhang (Figure 5). To avoid false attribution, each authorship is first stored as an AuthorInstance node. Deciding which of those instances are actually the same person is called clustering. It connects AuthorInstance nodes that likely belong to the same person with weighted PROBABLY_SAME_AS edges, using ORCID and other evidence. But that edge is an inference, not a fact. The system can revise it as new evidence comes in. Clustering actually runs twice; the first pass runs on the seed data alone so the pipeline knows which heavy-retraction authors are worth expanding into candidate papers; the second pass runs afterward, folding each new candidate paper’s AuthorInstance back into the right cluster. Figure 4 shows this second pass in action, connecting Dr. Kim’s AuthorInstance node on the candidate paper to her record on the retracted paper.

Facts and identity clusters are not a risk assessment yet. Next, they pass through a series of metadata-based sensors for various red flags. A scoring engine combines their outputs into a risk score. A reviewer can then select the top papers and submit them to the content-based analyses to gather more evidence.

## 2. Sensors and the scoring engine

Each sensor is a script dedicated to one red flag. It checks whether a candidate paper shows a specific suspicious sign and reports what it finds. Those findings are then written back into the candidate paper nodes as properties, for the scoring engine and downstream queries to read. Sensors are independent of each other. They’re plug-ins that can be added or removed. The metadata-based sensors raise circumstantial evidence, reasoning over the paper’s metadata already stored in the graph or reachable through API lookup. In contrast, three content-based sensors analyze the paper’s full text for the smoking guns. Because many subscription-based journals keep their full text behind a paywall, these three sensors can only run on the small set of publications where the full text is actually retrievable.

Here are four example metadata-based sensors:

- **Expression of Concern** — whether the paper carries a formal, dated, journal-issued Expression of Concern. This carries one of the heaviest weights in the score, since it's an adjudicated finding from an outside authority rather than something the system infers on its own.
- **ORI finding** — whether the paper is named in a formal misconduct finding by the U.S. Office of Research Integrity (ORI), the same body behind the Shin-Hee Kim case in section 1. Like Expression of Concern, this carries one of the heaviest weights, since it too is an adjudicated result from an outside authority.
- **Citing known-retracted work** — whether the paper cites any retracted work.
- **Author retraction history** — whether the paper’s own authors have other retracted papers. The system weighs a first or last author’s retraction more heavily than a middle author’s. The weight climbs further when that other retraction was due to misconduct rather than something benign, like Error in Data or Duplication of/in Article.

The rest of the metadata-based sensors check whether a paper’s venue has been delisted from Scopus or Web of Science, whether it carries a Crossref correction notice, and how high its journal’s, publisher’s, institution’s, and country’s retraction rates are.

The content-based sensors are the tortured-phrase and AI-text-tell detectors, plus a p-value-hacking scan. They run on the paper’s full text. Beyond the full-text sensors, PaperConan, the AI audit tool introduced above, offers one more in-depth check. It scans a paper’s raw supplementary data tables and spreadsheets for fabrication, such as duplicated values, impossible mean-and-standard-deviation combinations, or decimal digits whose frequencies are suspiciously uneven. Like the text sensors, it can only work where the paper’s content is public. Only 86 of the 796 not-yet-retracted candidate papers had such supplementary data files. Because PaperConan flags patterns, not causes, and those patterns often have innocent explanations, its results only enter the score once a person opens the actual data table and judges them by hand in a process called adjudication. Only two verdicts move the score: a confirmed fabrication carries the heaviest weight, and an anomaly that needs further human follow-up carries a smaller one.

The scoring engine turns all the red flags into a single score. Each flag has its own adjustable weight, and the engine sums the terms. For this proof of concept, the actual formula looks like this:

```
score = 3.0×retracted_citations + 2.0×external_retracted_citations
      + 2.5×tortured_phrases + 2.0×ai_text_tell + 0.5×p_value_hacking
      + 10.0×expression_of_concern + 4.0×ori_finding
      + Σ scaled_rate_terms + paperconan_bonus
```

Σ scaled_rate_terms sums two kinds of signals. The first is continuous retraction rates for the paper's institution, publisher, journal, and country. The second is for the paper's own authors. It asks whether any of them have another retraction at all. And separately, it asks what share of their total output those retractions represent. A handful of entities are always far worse than the rest, so each signal is minmax-scaled instead of flatly weighted, keeping outliers from dominating the ranking. That is, the single worst-in-corpus case earns the full target score, and everyone else is scored in proportion.

In practice, the scoring engine runs the formula twice. The first pass scores every candidate paper on the metadata-based terms alone, ranking them from highest risk to lowest. A reviewer then runs the content-based sensors and PaperConan by hand on the top of that first-pass ranking. Then the engine reruns the formula with the new evidence and updates the ranking.

## 4. Conclusion

Papers at Risk shows that an early-warning system for retraction risk is buildable today from data anyone can access. By turning Retraction Watch, OpenAlex, PubMed, the Federal Register, and DOAJ into a single knowledge graph, then layering metadata-based sensors, content-based sensors, and an adjudicated AI tool like PaperConan on top, the pipeline turns scattered signals into one ranked, explainable score. Even at this proof-of-concept scale, it surfaces real cases worth a reviewer’s attention well before any formal retraction notice would ever flag them.

The project’s weaknesses trace back mostly to its dependence on public data and human judgment. Content-based sensors and PaperConan only run where a paper’s full text or raw data tables are actually retrievable, which is a small fraction of the corpus. Just 86 of 796 candidate papers had usable supplementary data, so most of the score still rests on metadata alone. Author clustering can go wrong in both directions, missing a real match when the shared evidence falls just under the confidence threshold, or wrongly merging two different people when an outside source like OpenAlex mis-attributes an ORCID. Every weight in the scoring formula was also chosen by hand rather than fit against data, and a reviewer still has to select papers for the second round and adjudicate PaperConan’s findings by hand. In short, the system speeds up human triage but does not replace it.

The scoring engine’s terms carry a broader implication worth stating plainly. A candidate paper’s risk score depends not only on its own record, but also on the retraction history of its author, journal, publisher, and host country. This is based on the premise that misconduct clusters around specific actors and venues rather than occurring at random. This yields a useful signal, as a high retraction rate often points to systematic issues such as paper mill activity, peer review rings, or compromised editorial processes. However, this signal must be interpreted with caution, as a paper can inherit a high risk score purely through association rather than from any flaw in its own content. After all, Papers at Risk makes inferences but not verdicts or accusations.
