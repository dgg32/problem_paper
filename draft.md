Papers at Risk: Retraction Prognosis for Academic Publications
==============
***Using AI and a knowledge graph of the published literature to flag risky publications early***

Science moves forward by standing on the shoulders of past work, with every published paper serving as a Jenga block in that ever-rising tower. When a paper contains flawed data, invalid conclusions, or outright fraud though, the flaw pollutes downstream science and acts like a compromised block. If the block is not swiftly identified and pulled out, more and more research built on top of it risks crumbling. Studies of retraction timing put the median delay at close to two years, and throughout that window, unsuspecting researchers keep citing the flawed work, letting it propagate deep into the literature.

The scientific community is not entirely defenseless against this threat, but its safeguards are stationed at extreme ends of the process, and neither can scale to match the volume and speed of retraction-worthy papers. At one end is the Retraction Watch database, which now logs more than 71,000 records and has become the field’s system of record. It is indispensable, but it is downstream by construction: a ledger of verdicts already delivered, reporting conclusions rather than suspicions, so by the time a paper appears in it, the harm is already done. At the other end are independent sleuths, exemplified by figures like Geng Hongwei, known online by his handle “耿同学” (“Classmate Geng”), who left his PhD program in 2025 to become a full-time research-integrity whistleblower. Through public social media channels, he draws attention to data anomalies, prompting universities and journals to open formal investigations into suspect publications. Crowdsourced platforms like PubPeer serve a similar function, giving anonymous experts a place to flag image manipulation and statistical anomalies directly on a paper's page. But sleuths and platforms like these face two fundamental limitations of their own: manual review cannot keep pace with the sheer volume of modern publishing, and whistleblowers often pay a steep personal and professional price for stepping forward, making the approach fundamentally unsustainable. What is missing is an automated, scalable early-warning system, one that watches the literature at the pace it is actually produced and raises the alarm before the harm is done.

This project, Papers at Risk, is a step toward closing that gap, covering the parts of the literature where enough public metadata is available. It builds a knowledge graph of retracted and not-yet-retracted papers, runs the latter through a set of sensors, and scores each one's retraction risk. Those scores give human sleuths a clear set of leads to investigate further before a formal retraction notice ever appears. The code for this project is hosted on my GitHub repository here.

## 1. Architecture, data, and author clustering

Papers at Risk consists of a series of scripts, organized as three layers stacked on top of each other. The fact layer is a Neo4j knowledge graph that stores only verifiable facts about papers, journals, institutions, and authors. They are sourced directly from Retraction Watch, OpenAlex, or PubMed. The graph is then extended with candidate papers, which are not-yet-retracted work from authors with a heavy retraction history, for risk scoring. A second layer, the inference layer, sits on top. It first clusters author identities. Then the automatic metadata-based sensors and a weighted scoring engine reason over those facts, producing a risk score for each candidate paper. Its parameters, including clustering thresholds, sensor weights, and scoring methods, can be tuned, so its outputs are subjected to changes as the pipeline is recalibrated. A third, selective forensic layer sits above both. It is home to the content-based sensors, which a user can run on selected candidate papers to corroborate a suspicion the first two layers already raised. This small set of deep checks looks for things like numbers that don't add up in a paper's raw data tables and full-text scans for paper-mill fingerprints. These checks can only run on papers whose full text and supplementary data are present. Consequently, the third layer can only apply to a small subset of the candidate papers, a limitation section 2 comes back to. Together, the broad and forensic sensors give a reviewer a multi-faceted prognosis of a paper's retraction risk.

For this proof of concept, the graph is seeded from Retraction Watch’s Microbiology subject, about 2,100 records, which after data-hygiene checks yields around 1,600 retracted seed papers (Figure 2). For each of the 24 authors with the heaviest retraction history, the pipeline then pulls that author’s most-cited not-yet-retracted work from OpenAlex and PubMed, adding those specific papers and their citation edges as candidate papers. The result is a working graph of roughly 2,000 papers today. Those 2,100 Microbiology records are themselves a narrow slice of what's available: Retraction Watch’s broader biology subject runs to roughly 24,000 records, out of more than 71,000 in the database overall, and the architecture is designed so the same pipeline can scale the retracted-seed pool into that larger set and eventually the full dataset.

Treating the literature as a graph, rather than as a flat table, is what lets the system ask the questions that actually drive its risk score: “does this paper cite a paper that has already been retracted?” and “does this paper share an author-identity cluster with someone whose other paper was retracted for misconduct?” Answering either one means walking several hops, citation, then authorship, then identity, then retraction reason, in one query instead of a chain of joins.

One real case answers both at once. The U.S. Office of Research Integrity found that Dr. Shin-Hee Kim personally falsified and fabricated data across seven publications. The graph records her authorship of those seven papers, and also of one not-yet-retracted paper titled "Reverse Genetics for Newcastle Disease Virus as a Vaccine Vector." Figure 3 shows that this candidate paper cites one of the papers retracted for misconduct, and that the graph’s author-identity clustering links both papers to the same Shin-Hee Kim.


That link is compelling evidence, but it's worth being precise about what the graph itself is asserting here. The fact layer stops at facts like “this paper was retracted on this date for this reason” and “this author wrote this paper.” A paper’s own retraction reason can already point at misconduct, “Fabrication” or “Paper Mill,” for instance, since that reason is a sourced fact straight from Retraction Watch, not a judgment this system makes. What the graph itself never does is label a person as fraud: no author gets a fraud flag, only papers carry the sourced reasons already on record.

But “this author wrote this paper” hides a real complication: multiple different people can share the exact same name, in one extreme case eleven distinct researchers are all named Ying Zhang, so each authorship is first stored on its own, as an AuthorInstance node, rather than folded into a shared Author record. Deciding which of those instances are actually the same person is called clustering. It connects AuthorInstance nodes that likely belong to the same person, using ORCID and other evidence, with weighted PROBABLY_SAME_AS edges. That edge isn’t a sourced fact; it’s an inference the system can revise if new evidence changes the picture. Therefore, clustering belongs to the inference layer. It is exactly the mechanism behind Figure 3. Dr. Kim’s authorship of the candidate paper and her authorship of the retracted paper started out as two separate AuthorInstance nodes, and it’s clustering that connected them with the PROBABLY_SAME_AS edge shown in the figure.

Facts and identity clusters are not a risk assessment yet. They go through a series of metadata-based sensors for various red flags. A scoring engine weighs their outputs into a risk score. A reviewer can then select the top papers and submit them to the content-based analyses to gather more evidence. Section 2 walks through how these sensors and the scoring engine actually produce that score.

## 2. Sensors and the scoring engine

Each sensor is one script per red flag, answering a single narrow question and emitting evidence rather than a verdict. These sensors are independent of each other, and none of them renders a verdict on its own. They're also plug-ins, not a fixed list: each is a self-contained script, and a user can add or remove them from the Papers at Risk platform freely. The metadata-based sensors raise circumstantial evidence, reasoning over the paper's metadata already stored in the graph or reachable through API lookup. In contrast, three content-based sensors analyze the paper's full text and data tables for the smoking guns. Because many subscription-based journals keep their full text behind a paywall, these three sensors can only run on the small set of publications where it's actually retrievable.

Here are three example metadata-based sensors:

- **Expression of Concern** — whether the paper carries a formal, dated, journal-issued Expression of Concern.
- **Citing known-retracted work** — whether the paper cites any retracted work, either in-graph or, via a live OpenAlex lookup, outside the graph entirely.
- **Author retraction history** — whether the paper's own authors have other retracted work elsewhere, weighted higher for a first or last author than a middle one, and higher still when that other retraction's reason was misconduct rather than something more benign, like Error in Data or Duplication of/in Article.

The rest of the metadata-based sensors check whether a paper's venue has been delisted from Scopus or Web of Science, whether it carries a Crossref correction notice, and how its journal, publisher, institution, or country retraction rate compares to the rest of the corpus.

Turning that evidence into a single score is the job of scoring engine. Every sensor flag carries a weight, and those weights live in a single git-tracked YAML file rather than in code, so changing how much a signal matters, and seeing exactly who changed it and why, never requires touching a script. The weighting used in this proof of concept is only one way to balance these signals against each other; a different reviewer may reasonably strike that balance differently, and the architecture is built to let them do it without touching a line of code. The score a paper ends up with is a transparent sum of these weighted, independently-sourced facts, not a black-box verdict.

Some of those facts are themselves rates rather than yes/no flags, an institution's, publisher's, journal's, or author's own historical retraction rate, and rates like these are heavy-tailed: most entities sit near zero, while a handful sit far out on the tail. A flat weight on a raw rate either underweights the typical case or lets one extreme outlier swamp the ranking, so these signals are minmax-scaled instead: whichever entity is worst-in-corpus for that signal is anchored to a fixed target score, and every other entity is scored proportionally beneath it. A prolific author with well over a hundred retracted works to their name still only anchors the top of that one scale, rather than dominating every paper they ever touched by sheer volume.

A third group of checks sits apart from the rest because they cannot run on metadata alone. Paperconan's numeric forensics needs a paper's actual raw supplementary data tables, in a machine-readable spreadsheet, not a PDF, and the tortured-phrase and AI-text-tell detectors need to read the paper's actual full text, not just its title and abstract. Both depend on content that is often simply not public: a paper behind a paywall, or one whose supplementary files were never deposited anywhere fetchable, gives these sensors nothing to look at. That is the forensic layer: selective in that it can only reach the subset of papers whose underlying document happens to be retrievable, not the corpus as a whole. The measured gate is narrow. Of the 796 not-yet-retracted candidate papers checked, only 86, about one in nine, had supplementary data files paperconan could actually download and scan; the rest come back marked unavailable, not clean.

That gate is this project's real scope, and it is worth stating plainly rather than letting the ranked queue imply otherwise: this pipeline cannot assess an arbitrary paper. The metadata-only sensors still run on every paper in the graph, so an institution, publisher, or country with its own poor historical retraction rate can still nudge a score up a little even without a scrap of retrievable full text. But the sensors most capable of raising a genuine, paper-specific red flag, an ORI finding, a caliper-test scan for p-value hacking, a numeric audit of the actual data, a tortured-phrase hit in the actual prose, all need either a track record already on the public record or a document that is actually fetchable. If a paper's authors carry no prior retraction history, its journal has no flagged integrity problems, and it sits behind a paywall with no fetchable supplementary data, none of those higher-signal checks has anything to examine, and the paper will land near the bottom of the ranked queue by default, not because it was examined and found clean, but because there was no public data trail to examine in the first place. A low Tier-A score means "no evidence found within what this pipeline can see," never "this paper is fine." So the honest description of what Papers at Risk does is narrower than "flags risky papers": it flags risk where the sensors already have data to work with, which today is a small, unevenly distributed slice of the literature, open-access papers, papers with deposited supplementary files, authors and journals with an existing public track record, not a general-purpose scanner of everything published.

Not every signal the pipeline computes is allowed into that sum. PubPeer comment counts, a learned prior from a graph-neural-network classifier, and the raw severity counts from paperconan's numeric forensics on supplementary data tables are all computed, shown on the review card as context, and deliberately left out of the score itself. They are softer: opinion, a statistical prior, or an unadjudicated anomaly count, rather than a sourced fact, and the design keeps that distinction visible instead of letting it quietly collapse into one number.

That word "unadjudicated" matters. Paperconan's raw finding is only ever a starting point, never a verdict on its own. Before it can nudge a score, a person has to open the actual source table, the figure legend, and the paper's Methods section, and decide by hand whether an innocent explanation fits, a shared control, a re-plotted figure, a unit conversion, or none of the above. That verdict gets written by hand into the paper's own run record; no script ever assigns it. Two of its possible outcomes, confirmed and needs human, carry their own weight and rejoin the sum once written down. The rest, benign, false positive, inconclusive, needs more data, stay context-only, a record that someone looked and found nothing worth scoring. Nothing about this step happens automatically, on purpose. A plausible-looking anomaly only becomes a scored fact once a human has actually stood behind it.

The result is a ranked queue where a reviewer can trace every point back to the specific fact that produced it, and where nothing in the ranking claims more certainty than the evidence actually supports.
