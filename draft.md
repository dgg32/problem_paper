Papers at Risk: Retraction Prognosis for Academic Publications
==============
***Using a knowledge graph of the published literature and AI to flag risky publications early***

Science moves forward by standing on the shoulders of past work, with every published paper serving as a Jenga block in that ever-rising tower. When a paper contains flawed data, invalid conclusions, or outright fraud though, the flaw pollutes downstream science and acts like a compromised block. If the block is not swiftly identified and pulled out, more and more research built on top of it risks crumbling. Studies of retraction timing put the median delay at close to two years, and throughout that window, unsuspecting researchers keep citing the flawed work, letting it propagate deep into the literature.

The scientific community is not entirely defenseless against this threat, but its safeguards are stationed at extreme ends of the process, and neither can scale to match the volume and speed of retraction-worthy papers. At one end is the Retraction Watch database, which now logs more than 71,000 records and has become the field’s system of record. It is indispensable, but it is downstream by construction: a ledger of verdicts already delivered, reporting conclusions rather than suspicions, so by the time a paper appears in it, the harm is already done. At the other end are independent sleuths, exemplified by figures like Geng Hongwei, known online by his handle “耿同学” (“Classmate Geng”), who left his PhD program in 2025 to become a full-time research-integrity whistleblower. Through public social media channels, he draws attention to data anomalies, prompting universities and journals to open formal investigations into suspect publications. Crowdsourced platforms like PubPeer serve a similar function, giving anonymous experts a place to flag image manipulation and statistical anomalies directly on a paper's page. But sleuths and platforms like these face two fundamental limitations of their own: manual review cannot keep pace with the sheer volume of modern publishing, and whistleblowers often pay a steep personal and professional price for stepping forward, making the approach fundamentally unsustainable. What is missing is an automated, scalable early-warning system, one that watches the literature at the pace it is actually produced and raises the alarm before the harm is done.

This project, Papers at Risk, is a step toward closing that gap, covering the parts of the literature where enough public metadata is available. It builds a knowledge graph of retracted and not-yet-retracted papers, runs the latter through a set of sensors, and scores each one's retraction risk. Those scores give human sleuths a clear set of leads to investigate further, and an integrated AI audit tool is on hand to help. All of this happens before a formal retraction notice ever appears. The code for this project is hosted on my GitHub repository here.

## 1. Architecture, data, and author clustering

Papers at Risk consists of a graph and a series of scripts, organized as two layers stacked on top of each other. The fact layer is a Neo4j knowledge graph that stores only verifiable facts about papers, journals, institutions, and authors, sourced directly from places like Retraction Watch, OpenAlex, PubMed, the Federal Register, and DOAJ. The graph is then extended with candidate papers, which are not-yet-retracted work from authors with a heavy retraction history, for risk scoring. A second layer, the inference layer, sits on top and runs in two rounds. The first round clusters author identities, then runs the metadata-based sensors and a scoring engine produces an initial risk score for each paper. A reviewer then selects the highest-scoring candidates for a second round, where the content-based sensors analyze the full texts and the AI audit tool PaperConan scans the data tables. Because both depend on the paper’s full text or data actually being retrievable, this second round can only reach a small subset of the candidate papers, a limitation section 2 comes back to. The inference layer’s parameters, including clustering thresholds, sensor weights, and scoring methods, can all be tuned, so its outputs are subject to change as the pipeline is recalibrated. Together, the two scoring rounds give a reviewer a multi-faceted prognosis of a paper’s retraction risk.

For this proof of concept, the graph is seeded from Retraction Watch’s Microbiology subject, about 2,100 records, which after data-hygiene checks yields around 1,300 retracted seed papers (Figure 2). For each of the 24 authors with the heaviest retraction history, the pipeline then pulls that author’s most-cited not-yet-retracted work from OpenAlex and PubMed, adding those specific papers and their citation edges as candidate papers. The result is a working graph of roughly 2,000 papers today. Those 2,100 Microbiology records are themselves a narrow slice of what's available: Retraction Watch’s broader biology subject runs to roughly 24,000 records, out of more than 71,000 in the database overall, and the architecture is designed so the same pipeline can scale the retracted-seed pool into that larger set and eventually the full dataset.

Treating the literature as a graph, rather than as a flat table, is what lets the system ask the questions that actually drive its risk score: “does this paper cite a paper that has already been retracted?” and “does this paper share an author-identity cluster with someone whose other paper was retracted for misconduct?” Answering either one means walking several hops, citation, then authorship, then identity, then retraction reason, in one query instead of a chain of joins.

One real case answers both at once. The U.S. Office of Research Integrity found that Dr. Shin-Hee Kim personally falsified and fabricated data across seven publications. The graph records her authorship of those seven papers, and also of one not-yet-retracted paper titled "Reverse Genetics for Newcastle Disease Virus as a Vaccine Vector." Figure 3 shows that this candidate paper cites one of the papers retracted for misconduct, and that the graph’s author-identity clustering links both papers to the same Shin-Hee Kim.


That link is compelling evidence, but it's worth being precise about what the graph itself is asserting here. The fact layer stops at facts like “this paper was retracted on this date for this reason” and “this author wrote this paper.” A paper’s own retraction reason can already point at misconduct, “Fabrication” or “Paper Mill,” for instance, since that reason is a sourced fact straight from Retraction Watch, not a judgment this system makes. What the graph itself never does is label a person as fraud: no author gets a fraud flag, only papers carry the sourced reasons already on record.

But “this author wrote this paper” hides a real complication: multiple different people can share the exact same name, in one extreme case eleven distinct researchers are all named Ying Zhang, so each authorship is first stored on its own, as an AuthorInstance node, rather than folded into a shared Author record. Deciding which of those instances are actually the same person is called clustering. It connects AuthorInstance nodes that likely belong to the same person, using ORCID and other evidence, with weighted PROBABLY_SAME_AS edges. That edge isn’t a sourced fact; it’s an inference the system can revise if new evidence changes the picture. Therefore, clustering belongs to the inference layer. It is exactly the mechanism behind Figure 3. Dr. Kim’s authorship of the candidate paper and her authorship of the retracted paper started out as two separate AuthorInstance nodes, and it’s clustering that connected them with the PROBABLY_SAME_AS edge shown in the figure.

Facts and identity clusters are not a risk assessment yet. They go through a series of metadata-based sensors for various red flags. A scoring engine weighs their outputs into a risk score. A reviewer can then select the top papers and submit them to the content-based analyses to gather more evidence. The next section walks through how these sensors and the scoring engine actually produce that score.

## 2. Sensors and the scoring engine

Each sensor is a single script dedicated to one red flag. It checks whether a candidate paper shows that one suspicious sign and reports what it finds. Those findings are then written back into the graph as properties on the matching Paper node, so the scoring engine and downstream queries can read them directly. Sensors are independent of each other. They're plug-ins that can be added or removed. The metadata-based sensors raise circumstantial evidence, reasoning over the paper's metadata already stored in the graph or reachable through API lookup. In contrast, three content-based sensors analyze the paper's full text for the smoking guns. Because many subscription-based journals keep their full text behind a paywall, these three sensors can only run on the small set of publications where it's actually retrievable.

Here are four example metadata-based sensors:

- **Expression of Concern** — whether the paper carries a formal, dated, journal-issued Expression of Concern. This carries one of the heaviest weights in the score, since it's an adjudicated finding from an outside authority rather than something the system infers on its own.
- **ORI finding** — whether the paper is named in a formal misconduct finding by the U.S. Office of Research Integrity (ORI), the same body behind the Shin-Hee Kim case in section 1. Like Expression of Concern, this carries one of the heaviest weights, since it too is an adjudicated result from an outside authority.
- **Citing known-retracted work** — whether the paper cites any retracted work, either in-graph or, via a live OpenAlex lookup, outside the graph entirely.
- **Author retraction history** — whether the paper's own authors have other retracted work elsewhere, weighted higher for a first or last author than a middle one, and higher still when that other retraction's reason was misconduct rather than something more benign, like Error in Data or Duplication of/in Article.

The rest of the metadata-based sensors check whether a paper's venue has been delisted from Scopus or Web of Science, whether it carries a Crossref correction notice, and how its journal, publisher, institution, or country retraction rate compares to the rest of the corpus.

The content-based sensors are the tortured-phrase and AI-text-tell detectors, plus a p-value-hacking scan. They run on paper's full text. Beyond the full-text sensors, PaperConan, the AI audit tool introduced above, offers one more manual check. It scans a paper's actual raw supplementary data tables and spreadsheets for fabrication, such as duplicated values, impossible mean-and-standard-deviation combinations, or decimal digits whose frequencies are suspiciously uneven. Like the text sensors, it can only work where the paper's content is public. Only 86 of the 796 not-yet-retracted candidate papers had such supplementary data files; Because PaperConan flags patterns, not causes, and those patterns often have innocent explanations, its results only enter the score once a person opens the actual data table and judges them by hand, a process called adjudication. Only two verdicts move the score: a confirmed fabrication carries the heaviest weight, and an anomaly that still needs further human follow-up carries a smaller one.

The scoring engine turns all the red flags into a single score. Each flag enters as a term with its own adjustable weight, and the engine sums those terms. For this proof of concept, the actual formula looks like this:

```
score = 3.0×retracted_citations + 2.0×external_retracted_citations
      + 2.5×tortured_phrases + 2.0×ai_text_tell + 0.5×p_value_hacking
      + 10.0×expression_of_concern + 4.0×ori_finding
      + Σ scaled_rate_terms + paperconan_bonus
```

`Σ scaled_rate_terms` is the sum of the institution's, publisher's, journal's, or author's own historical retraction rates. Retraction rates are long-tailed. That is, most entities sit near zero, while a handful of worst offenders sit far out on the tail. So instead of a flat weight, these rate-based signals are minmax-scaled. Whichever entity is worst-in-corpus anchors a fixed target score, and every other entity is scored proportionally, so extreme outliers cannot overwhelm the ranking.

In practice, the scoring engine runs the formula twice. The first pass scores every candidate paper on the metadata-based terms alone, ranking them from highest risk to lowest. A reviewer then runs the content-based sensors and PaperConan by hand on the top of that ranking, and the engine reruns the formula with the new evidence to produce the final ranking.

## 3. Tests

To confirm the pipeline actually runs end to end on real data, not just in isolated unit tests, this section walks through one run on my own machine. The paper in this run is a real, not-yet-retracted candidate paper, and unlike Dr. Kim's case in section 1, no outside authority has adjudicated anything against its author. Publishing that author's name next to a numeric risk score would read as exactly the kind of accusation this project's own review banner warns against, "these are hypotheses for review, not accusations," so every identifying detail below, the paper's title, DOI, journal, publisher, and the author's name, is redacted here. Only the score breakdown itself, which is what this section is meant to demonstrate, is shown in full.

After running the graph build, the two rounds of sensors, and the scoring engine on the proof-of-concept graph from section 1, `build_review_page.py` renders the ranked results as a single static HTML file. Serving it locally with

```
python -m http.server 8899 --bind 127.0.0.1 --directory review
```

opens a review page bound to 127.0.0.1 only, so it never leaves my machine (Figure 4). Its header reports 793 candidate papers ranked, 227 of them carrying a formal Expression of Concern and 125 with PubPeer comments, and its own banner repeats the same caution raised in sections 1 and 2, "these are hypotheses for review, not accusations." A filter bar and a row of toggleable scoring-signal chips let a reviewer zero out any signal and re-rank live, so nothing in the score is hidden behind the number.

The top-ranked paper shows a total score of 30.11. Its title, DOI, journal, and author are redacted here. Expanding the card breaks that 30.11 down into its component terms. The Expression of Concern penalized the paper by +10.00. Its publisher carries the highest retraction rate anywhere in the corpus, adding another +10.00. The last author had one prior retraction due to misconduct, and that added another +6.67. Smaller terms round out the rest. They include the journal's and country's own retraction rates.
